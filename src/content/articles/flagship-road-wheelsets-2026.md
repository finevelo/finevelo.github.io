A flagship road disc wheelset costs about **$4,000** in 2026. Zipp's 353 NSW and 454 NSW launched at **$4,000** in their 2021 generation; the 2025 versions add up to **$4,300** and **$4,200** as two single wheels in SRAM's US shop; Cadex's Max 50 is **$3,999**, Enve's SES 6.7 Pro **$3,750**, and Roval's Rapide CLX III **$3,400**. Claimed weights range from **1,120 g** (Scope Artech 4) to about **1,500 g** (Enve SES 6.7 Pro, 59/65 mm).

Price no longer tracks weight closely. Lightweight's Meilenstein EVO is the outlier at $7,590 (US retailer) for a claimed 1,380 g. It is neither the lightest nor the deepest.

## Why are carbon spokes spreading?

Carbon spokes are the clearest technical change at the top of the market:

- **Roval** moved the Alpinist CLX III to composite spokes and saved 134 g over the previous version. The Rapide CLX III also uses Arris carbon-composite spokes.
- **Cadex** bonds its carbon spokes straight into the hub flanges of the Max 40 and Max 50.
- **Lightweight** uses full-carbon spokes on the Meilenstein EVO.
- **Shimano** brought carbon spokes to Dura-Ace with the R9370 wheels in July 2026; the C50 set is claimed at 1,302 g.

Enve still uses Alpina steel spokes on its SES 6.7 Pro and 4.5 Pro, both flagships. A carbon-spoked wheel is lighter and stiffer, but harder to repair at the roadside: a broken bonded spoke, as on Cadex's wheels, usually means sending the wheel back to the maker, although Roval ships the Rapide CLX III with four spare spokes.

## Hooked or hookless: has the industry decided?

No. Under the ETRTO standard, hookless rims are limited to **5 bar (72.5 psi)**, and the debate about tyre retention has continued since high-profile crashes on hookless rims.

- **Hookless:** Zipp 353 NSW and 454 NSW, Cadex Max 40 and Max 50, Enve SES 4.5 Pro.
- **Hooked:** Enve SES 6.7 Pro (rated to 100 psi), DT Swiss ARC 1100, Roval CLX III, Princeton, Scope Artech 4.

With wider rims (21–25 mm internal) and 28–30 mm tyres, most riders run pressures well below 72.5 psi anyway. For heavier riders, or anyone who wants to run narrower tyres hard, a hooked rim still keeps more options open.

## What does the UCI's 65 mm rule change?

Since **1 January 2026**, rims deeper than **65 mm** are banned in UCI mass-start road races, a measure the UCI justifies with rider safety at rising race speeds. Enve replaced its 60/66 mm SES 6.7 with the 59/65 mm SES 6.7 Pro. DT Swiss still lists an 85 mm ARC 1100, which can no longer be used in mass-start road races. Velo notes that WorldTour riders on road stages average about 50 mm anyway, which is where most of the flagships above sit.

## Is the warranty part of the price?

At $4,000, crash cover matters as much as grams:

- **Zipp:** lifetime warranty on 2021-and-later wheels, which also covers impact damage sustained while riding. Other damage is handled through a paid replacement programme.
- **Enve:** lifetime warranty against defects for the original owner (five years for second owners), plus crash replacement for rims free for the first three years and at 30% off list price after that.
- **Roval:** lifetime warranty against defects and two years of free crash replacement (terms differ between the US and UK).
- **Bontrager:** lifetime warranty for the original owner under its Carbon Care programme.

## Are $4,000 wheels worth it?

They are worth it when your bike came with mid-range wheels. Wheels are then still the biggest upgrade you can make. If your bike already came with the brand's top wheels, like the Roval Rapide on an S-Works Tarmac SL9, a change mostly buys a different trade-off between depth, weight and rim type, not a measurable gain. TOUR's 2025 wind-tunnel tests found that the biggest remaining gains lie in tyres rather than frames and wheels: Continental's Aero 111 on the front wheel was worth up to four watts.
